Automation, AI & Employment

A Comparative Study of the United States and China
By Mevis Tan Qian Ying | Summer 2026

Introduction

As we are currently living in a highly technology advancement world, the choices we made and resources we undertake are likely shaped by this factor presented in our lives. The use of Artificial intelligence (AI) has always been mentioned as a double-edge sword. On one hand, it provided us a range of benefits; ranging from enhancing efficiency at work to improving military capabilities among nations. On the other hand, it raises the question of accountability and causes an impact on employment structures. The worldwide employment landscape is changing as AI technologies become more widely used. As reported by Goldman Sachs, while there is a possibility that AI capabilities may lead to structural unemployment, "technology change tends to boost demand for workers in new occupations" (Sachs, 2025).

Hence, this paper seeks to examine the role of automation and AI in transforming employment structures in US and China respectively. In today's world, while both the US and China are leading in developing AI pathways, each country differs in economic systems and labour markets. This paper will start by highlighting key economics concept under the theoretical framework, followed by underlining how automation and AI influences employment structures in US and China respectively through comparing case studies. Subsequently, the paper will evaluate the differences and argue that although both the US and China are undergoing employment restructuring, the primary driver of this change in China is the automation of manufacturing processes, while in the US it is driven by AI-powered changes to tasks in certain work sectors. The paper will conclude by summarising all main points and answer what this means for the future and in terms of policymaking.

"Although both the US and China are undergoing employment restructuring, the primary driver of this change in China is the automation of manufacturing processes, while in the US it is driven by AI-powered changes to tasks in certain work sectors."

Theoretical Framework

Joseph Schumpeter's creative destruction theory suggested that creative destruction, which also refers to an 'imbalance', rather than market equilibrium, is the primary driving force behind capitalism. He coined the expression "creative destruction" in 1942 to explain how innovative capitalist products and methods always supplant older ones (McCraw, 2007). Technological advancement often challenges traditional production processes, firms, and job kinds, thus resulting in economic restructuring and long-term growth.

Applying Schumpeter's creative destruction theory in this paper is important as it allows us to understand the significance of automation and artificial intelligence in reshaping employment structures in US and China. For instance, in the US, private technology innovation companies such as Google announced restructuring to its company in order to accelerates and execute its AI initiatives. According to a news report, "the Assistant teams focusing on devices and home experiences will be integrated into the Platforms & Devices division. This reorganization aims to align these teams more closely with the product surfaces they are developing for and consolidate AI smart home initiatives at Google under one umbrella" (Daws, 2024). This demonstrates that corporations in the United States are progressively allocating resources and focusing on the AI area. Simultaneously, the emergence of new businesses such as cloud computing and AI research creates new high-skilled jobs. The US model thus represents market-driven creative destruction, in which innovation occurs quickly, frequently exceeding regulatory and labour market adaption.

In China, creative destruction takes place within a more state-controlled system. For example, government programs such as "Made in China 2025 (MIC2025)" and the Next Generation Artificial Intelligence Development Plan (NGAIDP) demonstrated government's plan in pushing for industry upgrading and improvement. As stated by World Economic Forum (WEF), "China has continued to expand its industrial and technological footprint" (Kuo, 2025). As a result, low-wage manufacturing jobs are being automated, while new employment opportunities develop in e-commerce logistics and AI services. Schumpeter's theory is important because it gives a prism through which to interpret economic restructuring as an unavoidable consequence of innovation.

Human capital theory serves as an important tool in analysing the employment restructuring. Human Capital Theory, first developed by Gary Becker, regards education, training, and skills as kinds of capital investment that increase workers' productivity and earning capacity (Weiss, 2015). This theory helps us understand that employment restructuring is not just driven by technological factors, such as AI and automation, but rather due to education. The ability for workers to adapt through learning ultimately leads us to question whether automation remains a key factor in resulting widespread unemployment or causes a growth that is driven by innovation. This perspective is significant as it connects the adoption of technology to the policy aspects of economic resilience.

In this paper, human capital theory explains why workers and economies adapt better to technological changes as compared to others. For example, in US, there is an increase in companies adopting automation in manufacturing jobs. Hence, individuals with science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) backgrounds are continuously in demand, whilst those in monotonous, low-skilled employment are displaced. The growing skills in-demand between high and low educated workers demonstrates how unequal access to human capital significantly influences job results. The Chinese government on the other hand views the development of human capital as an important base for industrial improvement, and it has since established policies to promote technical education and vocational training, with the goal of preparing individuals for advancing into manufacturing and digital sectors.

Impact of Automation and AI on US Employment

Artificial intelligence is frequently regarded as a job killer; but, on the other hand, it provides opportunities for both companies and individuals, ultimately influencing employment structure in our economy. The influence of automation and AI on employment in the US is uniquely defined by the country's economic structure and technological market development. According to a news report by the MIT, jobs in the financial and business sectors "shrank by about 2% to 2.5% over five years, because they have a high share of tasks that match what AI can do" (Murray, 2025), which suggests that these sectors are most impacted by AI as certain tasks are being replaced with automation.

For instance, financial sectors face increased pressure to deliver value effectively, hence adopting technologies that helps with streamlining basic tasks in the work processes allows employees to focus more on making better decisions instead of doing unnecessary repetitive work (Downie & O'Brien, 2025). Instead of eliminating entire occupations in the financial sector, AI frequently restructures what employees perform inside their positions. As the US continues to lead in generative AI and machine learning, such technologies systems adopted by the US enable automation in repetitive tasks and heavy tasks involving data. Thus, administrative assistants and entry-level white-collar workers are the occupations that may be restructured as a result of automation, rather than industrial workers.

This can be seen supported by an article published by the World Economic Forum (WEF) which highlighted that "While 170 million new jobs are projected to be created this decade, the rise of AI-powered tools threatens to automate as many roles as it creates, particularly for white collar, entry-level roles. Bloomberg finds that AI could replace more than 50% of the tasks performed by market research analysts (53%) and sales representatives (67%), compared to just 9% and 21% for their managerial counterparts" (Leopold, 2025). As a result, while there is a decrease in such jobs, there is also an increase in jobs relating to AI, such as AI operations specialists and data scientist.

The case study of Amazon and its creation of chatbots and virtual assistants' technologies aimed to process queries faster, automate tasks, obtain meaningful insights, and improve corporate performance. The world's largest online retail company, Amazon, embedded workplace process automation through integrating large language models (LLM) as one of their AI strategies. As internal services like as HR and procurement become more reliant on AI technologies for summarization and coordination, the number of entry-level customer service agents declines, thereby influencing the workforce. As reported in a news article, "Amazon is communicating a message we have been increasingly hearing from other technology companies – AI is progressing so fast in improving productivity that the need for hiring will diminish over time" (Reuters, 2025).

Impact of Automation and AI on China Employment

China, on the other hand, while also being regarded worldwide as having strong technology innovation, its experiences contrast dramatically from that of the US due to its massive manufacturing workforce and rapid industrial automation. China's employment has been heavily focusing on manufacturing, such that the majority of people are employed in automotive manufacturing jobs and machinery. These industries are most vulnerable to robotic automation as repetitive manual tasks rarely changes in steps, thus are easier being integrated into robotic systems. As mentioned in an article, "China has rapidly become a global leader in automation. From 2018 to 2020, a sales increase between 15 and 20 percent on average per year is possible for industrial robots" (Heer, 2017), which suggested that robotic automation are increasingly making an impact in China's employment and economy.

China has become the world's largest buyer of industrial robots, spurred by factors such as an increased labour costs in coastal areas, government subsidies for automation technology, and the need to meet modern manufacturing requirements. As a result, automation in China's sectors frequently replaces tasks like quality control and packing. Meanwhile, it boosts demand for vocations like robot technicians and industrial AI system integrators. This can be seen supported in a news article which highlighted "With nearly 80% of local companies expected to adopt some form of automation by year's end, factories are upgrading machines and rewriting workflows — often faster than their workers can adapt" (Fan & Chen, 2025).

As a result, low-skill manufacturing employment is declining as robots enhance productivity. In contrast, it also highlighted concerns about increased regional inequality across provinces and limiting labour mobility as a result of China's household registration system. Compared to the US, automation in China can be seen as more frequently replaces the entire role and its previous functions due to the nature of factory work, rather than the case of AI restructuring an employee duty.

Chinese multinational home appliance and consumer electronics manufacturer company, Haier, has been leading in promoting smart manufacturing as a result of AI. As it deals with the constant transformation of the internet era and the shift from mass manufacturing to mass customization, Haier Group has been consistently prioritizing users' experience by building the industry-leading intelligent manufacturing system through real-time interconnection processes, which thus fulfils user personalization experiences. As reported by Haier company, the Shenyang interconnection factory "took the lead to build the seamless intelligent manufacturing system of enterprise and the data that the users need by establishing automation and intelligent production line and building informatization and digital information system. Also, it implemented the intelligent production based on real-time data sharing" (Haier, 2025).

For instance, "the workers just need to put the door of refrigerator into the cage randomly and the production line will automatically retrieve according to the models ordered by customers" (Haier, 2025). This demonstrated the success of Haier company switching to automation. However, while this itself is a success, the number of employees may significantly decrease as a result of automation. Many repetitive tasks can now be handled by machines more efficiently which droves the reduction in headcount employment. This case study illustrates the dual nature of automation, which is while it enable employer to tackle mundane tasks efficiently and reduce labour costs, employee may be at a disadvantage end such that their primarily roles and jobs may be completely eliminated.

Evaluation

Both the US and China case studies on how automation and AI affect employment structures respectively demonstrated how changes in technology reshape employment structures in various ways, emphasizing both the disruptive and creative dimensions of innovation. A comparison of the two countries shows that the sectoral composition and policy environment does shape the nature of technological impact.

For instance, in the US, while AI adoption driven by market forces promotes innovation and transformation of tasks, the existence of fragmented retraining systems and social protections renders some groups vulnerable. In the US, the adoption of AI is primarily focused on the finance sector and corporate operations, including retail and technology companies. These companies embraced generative AI and machine learning. AI primarily restructures tasks rather than eliminating entire occupations, augmenting some roles while displacing certain routine clerical and administrative functions. According to Schumpeter creative destruction theory, traditional jobs may be lost, but new positions and demands are arising in areas such as software development and digital analytics.

In China, however, while the government-driven industrial approach speeds up the adoption of robots and increases productivity, it poses a risk of worsening inequality across regions and labour displacement if human capital is not improved swiftly. Schumpeter creative destruction theory can also be seen applying in the case of China, such as the case study of Haier company. The cases demonstrated that automation and AI do not uniformly decrease employment, but rather reshape labour demand. This process of creative destruction creates new opportunities for workers with relevant skills, while leaving others vulnerable. In the end, the comparative assessment highlights the significance of investing in human capital and enacting policies that facilitate worker transition, as these are vital for converting technological disruption into enduring economic and social benefits.

Conclusion

This paper has argued that automation and AI are transforming employment structures in both the US and China, however the nature of these transformations and driving factors are fundamentally different. From a theoretical perspective, the trends in both US and China exemplify Schumpeter creative destruction theory, where technological innovation concurrently eradicates some job opportunities while generating others. The Human Capital theory is important in this paper as it highlighted the distribution of skills among workers dictates who reaps the benefits of technology.

Ultimately, the comparative case studies and analysis of the US and China in this paper shows that technological change is not intrinsically good or bad, but rather transformative. It generates opportunities and challenges, which are contingent on how societies handle workforce adaptation. To convert the benefits of automation and AI into inclusive economic growth and equip workers for the next technological upheaval, it is vital for policymakers to ensure that human capital development and industrial policy strategies are effective and practical.

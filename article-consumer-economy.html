Beyond "Made in China": The Rise of China’s Consumer Economy

By Catherine OmartinsSummer 2026

When people think about China's economic rise, the first image that often comes to mind is a factory. For decades, "Made in China" became almost synonymous with mass production. China's manufacturing capacity, enormous workforce and integration into global supply chains transformed it into one of the world's most important economic powers. Products made in China travelled across the world, shaping markets and making the country an indispensable part of global trade.

But China's economic story has continued to evolve. Today, China is not only producing goods for the world. It is increasingly creating the brands, platforms and business models through which people around the world consume.

This transformation is visible in companies such as Huawei, Xiaomi, BYD and Haier, as well as platforms such as Alibaba and ByteDance. These companies represent a China that is different from the one captured by the phrase "Made in China." They are not simply manufacturers. They are companies developing their own technologies, brands, consumer ecosystems and methods of reaching global markets.

"China is not only producing goods for the world. It is increasingly creating the brands, platforms and business models through which people consume."

From Factory to Brand

China's rise as a manufacturing power was built on its ability to produce goods efficiently and at scale. But becoming a global consumer economy requires something more.

A company that manufactures a product for another brand does not necessarily need to understand the consumer who eventually buys it. A company building its own brand does.

It must understand what consumers value, how they make purchasing decisions, what they are willing to pay, how they interact with technology and what makes them return.

Chinese companies have increasingly developed these capabilities within one of the world's largest consumer markets.

China's enormous domestic market has provided companies with an environment in which they can test products, refine business models and compete at scale before expanding abroad. The result is a growing group of Chinese companies whose competitive advantage is no longer based solely on manufacturing.

It is based on their ability to combine production, technology, branding, distribution and consumer understanding.

This is an important shift.

China is increasingly moving from being a place where products are made to a place where ideas about how products should be created, marketed and consumed are developed.

The distinction may seem subtle, but it changes how we understand China's global economic influence.

A product manufactured in China represents one form of economic power.

A business model developed in China and adopted elsewhere represents another.

Understanding the Chinese Consumer

Perhaps the most important part of this transformation is the Chinese consumer themselves.

China's economic development has created a vast and increasingly sophisticated consumer market. At the same time, Chinese consumers have experienced rapid changes in technology, retail and digital commerce.

Shopping on mobile phones, digital payments, food delivery, online marketplaces and livestream commerce have become deeply integrated into everyday life. The boundary between physical and digital consumption has become increasingly fluid.

What makes this particularly interesting is the speed at which consumers can adopt new products and platforms.

Chinese businesses therefore operate in an environment where convenience, affordability, speed and technological integration can be essential to competition.

But Chinese consumer culture cannot simply be reduced to a willingness to buy inexpensive products.

Consumers can be highly responsive to branding, trends, convenience and innovation. A business must continually give people a reason to choose it in a market where alternatives are often readily available.

This competitive environment has encouraged companies to experiment with different ways of attracting and retaining consumers.

It has also produced an interesting relationship between technology and everyday consumption.

Technology in China is not always experienced as something separate from ordinary life. It can be embedded in the act of ordering food, paying for a drink, purchasing clothing, communicating with friends or discovering a new product.

The result is a consumer environment in which businesses can collect information, respond quickly to demand and constantly refine their offerings.

China's consumer economy is therefore not simply about what people buy.

It is also about how businesses have learned to understand and respond to the people buying from them.

The Economics of Making Affordability Scalable

One company that helped me understand this dynamic is Mixue.

During my time in China, I became a frequent customer. What initially attracted me was simple: the products were inexpensive, accessible and easy to find.

But what interested me later was the business behind the product.

Mixue demonstrates how affordability can become a strategy for scale. Its model combines relatively simple products with franchising, centralised procurement, supply-chain coordination and a highly recognisable brand. By the end of 2025, the company had expanded to nearly 60,000 stores globally.

What I found most interesting was not simply that the drinks were cheap. It was that the price made repeated consumption possible.

A consumer does not need to think carefully before making a small purchase. And when a product is sufficiently accessible, familiar and convenient, an occasional purchase can become part of a person's routine.

This reveals something important about the economics of mass consumption.

Scale does not always have to come from selling expensive products to a small number of people. It can also come from making relatively inexpensive products available to very large numbers of people, very frequently.

But affordability alone does not explain Mixue's expansion.

The business also relies on standardisation and a franchise structure that allows its model to be reproduced across locations. Behind the simplicity of the product is a more complex system of procurement, distribution and brand management.

That is what makes Mixue interesting as a case study.

It demonstrates how a Chinese company can take something ordinary and build an enormous business around the principles of accessibility, repetition and scale.

More importantly, it demonstrates that China's consumer economy is not necessarily defined by luxury or technological sophistication.

Sometimes its strength lies in making ordinary things more accessible to more people.

When Chinese Business Models Go Global

The significance of these developments becomes clearer when Chinese companies expand beyond China.

For many years, China's international economic presence was primarily associated with the export of manufactured goods. Today, Chinese companies are increasingly taking their brands, technologies, platforms and business models into international markets.

This can be seen across sectors including automobiles, telecommunications, consumer electronics, e-commerce, fashion and food and beverage.

Going global, however, is fundamentally different from exporting.

A company entering a foreign market has to understand consumers who may have different purchasing habits, cultural expectations, income levels and preferences. It must determine what should remain consistent with its original identity and what must be adapted.

This makes international expansion an important test of the capabilities Chinese companies have developed at home.

It also creates an interesting form of economic exchange.

When a Chinese company enters another country, it does not necessarily bring only a product. It can bring a way of organising production, distribution, franchising, technology and consumer interaction.

This is particularly significant for emerging markets, where Chinese companies may encounter consumers with very different needs from those in China but with similar demands for affordability, convenience and accessibility.

The global expansion of Chinese consumer businesses therefore raises a broader question:

Are Chinese companies exporting products, or are they beginning to export approaches to doing business?

The answer may increasingly be both.

When Commerce Becomes Cultural Exchange

There is also a cultural dimension to the globalisation of Chinese consumer businesses that is easy to overlook.

I did not initially become interested in Chinese consumer culture because I was consciously studying China's soft power. I simply lived there.

I ate the food. I used Chinese platforms. I shopped online. I visited businesses. I became familiar with brands. I heard the language every day.

These ordinary experiences changed the way I understood China.

That is why I believe commerce can become an unexpected form of cultural exchange.

A person does not necessarily need to study China to become familiar with it.

A brand can create curiosity about where it came from. A food product can introduce someone to unfamiliar tastes. A digital platform can expose users to new forms of communication and entertainment. A business can make aspects of another society feel ordinary long before a person consciously thinks of them as cultural experiences.

A product can create familiarity before a person ever consciously decides to learn about the country behind it.

This does not mean that every Chinese brand becomes an instrument of cultural influence. But the accumulation of everyday encounters can change how a country is perceived.

China's global presence is therefore increasingly experienced not only through diplomacy or major infrastructure projects, but through ordinary acts of consumption.

This is a different kind of global influence.

It is quieter.

It happens when someone reaches for a phone, orders something online, enters a store or recognises a brand without necessarily thinking about the country behind it.

Beyond "Made in China"

The rise of China's consumer economy reflects a broader transformation in the country's economic development.

China is still a manufacturing power. It remains deeply integrated into global production and supply chains. But that is no longer enough to describe the full extent of its economic capabilities.

China is simultaneously a manufacturing centre, a technological power, a massive consumer market, a source of global brands and an increasingly important creator of business models.

The significance of this transformation extends beyond individual companies.

It changes the way China participates in the global economy.

A country that once became globally important by producing for others is increasingly becoming globally influential by creating for itself and exporting what it creates.

Its companies are learning how to compete not only on cost, but on branding, technology, consumer understanding, convenience and scale.

And perhaps the most interesting part of this transformation is that it can be observed through the most ordinary things.

A cup of tea.

A smartphone.

A shopping app.

A delivery order.

These products may appear unrelated to the larger story of China's rise. But together, they reveal something important about the country's transformation.

China's economic influence is no longer only about what the world buys from China.

It is increasingly about what the world experiences, adopts and learns from Chinese businesses.

My experience in China began with an education in economics and trade. Living there, however, taught me that economies are not experienced only through statistics, trade balances or policy documents.

They are experienced through everyday life.

And perhaps that is why the next chapter of China's global economic story will not be written only in factories or government offices.

It will also be written in the places where people shop, consume, interact and choose what becomes part of their daily lives.

China is no longer simply making for the world. Increasingly, it is creating for the world.

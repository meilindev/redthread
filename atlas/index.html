About this atlas

The Fifty-Six

China recognises fifty-six minzu — nationalities. The number is exact, official, and not a fact about the world. It is the result of a specific bureaucratic project carried out in the 1950s, and this map is an argument about where its lines fall awkwardly.

How the fifty-six were made

When the first census of the People's Republic was taken in 1953–54, citizens were invited to register their nationality. More than four hundred distinct names came back, over two hundred of them from Yunnan alone. That was politically unworkable: the new state had promised regional autonomy and guaranteed representation to its minority peoples, and it could not administer four hundred of them.

So from 1954 teams of linguists, ethnologists and historians were sent into the field to sort the submissions — the Ethnic Classification Project, minzu shibie. Their nominal framework was Stalin's four criteria for a nation: common language, common territory, common economic life, common psychological character. In practice the criteria could not be applied mechanically, and the anthropologists running the work — Fei Xiaotong foremost among them — said so at the time. Language did most of the real work, with historical and political judgement filling the gaps.

Thirty-eight nationalities were recognised by 1954, fifty-three by 1964, the Lhoba in 1965, and the Jino in 1979. The list has not changed since. Several hundred thousand people are still recorded in the census as undistinguished, belonging to no official category at all.

Why the lines are awkward

Four hundred names became fifty-six boxes, and the compression left marks. Some categories hold peoples who share neither language nor religion nor history: the Yugur are two peoples speaking unrelated Turkic and Mongolic languages; the Yao span three separate language families; the Hui are defined by religion alone, so a Chinese-speaking Muslim in Gansu and an Austronesian-speaking Cham in Hainan share a category. Elsewhere one people was cut in two by a provincial boundary — the Mosuo are Naxi in Yunnan and Mongol in Sichuan.

Those cases are what this map breaks out into separate markers. The eighty-one dots are not a rival classification. They are the fifty-six with the seams shown.

It cuts the other way too. The categories are seventy years old now, they carry real entitlements, and people identify with them. A label assigned by a work team in 1954 is, by 2026, simply who someone is.

Where the data comes from

Two different kinds of content sit in this atlas, and they deserve different levels of trust.

The geometry is real and sourced. Province, prefecture and county boundaries come from DataV.GeoAtlas; rivers, coastlines, landmasses and the 427 settlements behind the heat map come from Natural Earth, which is public domain. Both are simplified but not invented. Population figures are the 2020 national census. Basemap imagery and relief are Esri's.

The writing is not sourced. Every descriptive entry — the peoples, the landforms, the trade goods, the industrial clusters, the dwellings — was written from general knowledge and carries no citations. It is intended to be accurate and is not a substitute for a source. Treat it as an informed sketch, and check anything you intend to rely on.

Known limits

  • A marker is a centre of gravity, not a territory. Almost every people here lives interleaved with others, and no dot implies a boundary.
  • Population figures for groups split out of an official category are estimates, and are marked as such.
  • Provincial minority shares are approximate.
  • County-level population data is not included; the county layer shows the administrative map of ethnic autonomy instead.
  • The nine-dash line ships in the source boundary data and has been deliberately excluded: it is a disputed maritime claim, not an administrative border.
  • Several subjects here — classification itself, Xinjiang, Tibet — are contested, and reasonable sources disagree. Where that is true the entries try to say so rather than pick a side.

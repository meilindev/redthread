China in Algeria: Contracts, Finance & Flagship Projects

Algeria has been Africa's #1 destination for Chinese construction-contract revenue in 11 of the last 27 years. This tracks the money, the megaprojects, and where it fits against its North African neighbors, 1998–2024. Cross-country comparison lives on the Compare tab.

Aid is a side channel, not the main story. AidData's Global Chinese Development Finance Dataset tracks $310.6M of aggregable Chinese government grants and loans to Algeria, 2000–2021 — driven mostly by one-off grants and loans for individual buildings (an opera house, a foreign ministry office, youth culture centers, a state telecom project) plus COVID-era vaccine donations, not sustained development financing — versus the tens of billions in commercial construction-contract revenue on the Overview tab. Algeria's oil wealth means it hires Chinese state-owned contractors directly rather than borrowing from China to pay for infrastructure, unlike many lower-income African states. Totals here count only AidData's aggregable records, which is why the cancelled $3.4B El Hamdania port on the Flagship projects tab isn't included — see Notes & method for why.

How to read these numbers

This site draws on two datasets that count different things, over different periods, in different dollars. Most of the apparent contradictions between tabs come from those differences rather than from disagreement about the facts. The important ones are below.

Why one cancelled port is bigger than all official finance combined

The Flagship projects tab shows the El Hamdania port at $3.4B in pledged China Eximbank financing. The Official financing tab puts all Chinese official finance to Algeria at $310.6M. Both are correct.

AidData marks each record with a Recommended For Aggregates flag, and excludes records that would double-count or that never became real money — pledges and commitments that were announced but never disbursed. El Hamdania is precisely that case: pledged in 2017, suspended in 2019, formally shelved in 2025. Not a dollar of it moved.

Every total on the Official financing tab uses only aggregable records. Counting all 57 records regardless of status would give $4.23B — a figure that mostly measures announcements rather than transfers, which is why it isn't the headline. The port still earns its place on the projects tab, because a $3.4B project collapsing is itself the story.

“Contract revenue” is not the same as contracts signed

The SAIS-CARI figures are gross annual revenue booked by Chinese construction firms on work performed in Algeria that year — closer to an income statement than to a deal announcement. A single megaproject shows up spread across many years as it is built, not as one spike when it is signed.

This is why the flagship project markers on the Overview chart line up with sustained plateaus rather than one-year jumps, and why Algeria's revenue stays high through the 2010s while the highway and airport were under construction.

The two datasets cover different periods

Contract revenue runs 1998–2024. Official finance runs 2000–2021, because AidData's version 3.0 tracks commitments only through 2021. Comparisons between the two are therefore directional, not year-matched — the gap between commercial contracting and official finance is large enough (roughly two orders of magnitude) that the three missing years don't change the conclusion.

Dollars are not all the same dollars

Contract revenue is nominal, unadjusted US$ as reported in China's statistical yearbooks. Official finance is constant 2021 US$, inflation-adjusted by AidData. Flagship project values are as-reported at the time, with the original currency noted where the source used euros or renminbi rather than dollars.

Practically: the cumulative $100.4B contract figure is a sum of nominal dollars across 27 years, so it overstates recent years relative to early ones in real terms. It is presented that way because it is how the source publishes it.

The megaprojects are mostly absent from the aid data — deliberately

The Grand Mosque, the East–West Highway, and the airport terminal do not appear in AidData's Algeria records, because they were not Chinese-financed. Algeria paid for them from hydrocarbon revenue and hired Chinese state-owned firms as contractors. That distinction is the site's central argument: Algeria is a major customer of Chinese construction, not a major borrower from Chinese state lenders.

Limitations

  • Contract revenue is reported by China's own statistical agencies and is not independently audited. SAIS-CARI notes that its country sum and the published Africa total disagree in some years.
  • The four flagship projects are a curated selection of the largest and most documented, not a complete inventory of Chinese construction in Algeria.
  • Flagship project values come from press and corporate reporting and vary between sources; where estimates conflict, the range is shown on the card rather than a single number.
  • Only five North African countries are shown for comparison. Rank and share figures, however, are computed against all 56 African countries in the source data.

Reproducing this

Every figure is derived programmatically from the two source files rather than transcribed by hand; data.js is generated output. Both datasets are public and free:

  • SAIS-CARI — Chinese construction contract revenue in Africa (updated November 2025)
  • AidData — Global Chinese Development Finance Dataset, v3.0

The full underlying tables, including every AidData record with its status and flow type, are browsable and downloadable as CSV on the Explore data tab.

Data: SAIS China-Africa Research Initiative, Johns Hopkins SAIS (construction contract revenue, updated Nov 2025) and AidData Global Chinese Development Finance Dataset v3.0 (official finance, 2000–2021), plus project-level reporting cited on the Flagship projects tab. All dollar figures are nominal/current unless noted.
